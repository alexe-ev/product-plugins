## Input

Product: Finly — a B2C mobile app for personal finance.

Research conducted: 10 user interviews (6 churned users — no session after day 7; 4 retained users — 3+ sessions in first two weeks). All participants completed onboarding and connected at least one bank account.

Research questions: (1) Why do users disengage after week 1? (2) What moments feel genuinely valuable? (3) What progress signals, if any, motivate return visits?

Interview notes summary:

## Expected output

Research questions: (1) Why do users disengage after week 1? (2) What moments feel genuinely valuable? (3) What progress signals motivate return visits?

Key themes:

Theme 1 — Judgment vs. progress framing: Churned users experienced the app as surfacing failure (overspend, unmet budgets). Retained users experienced it as surfacing progress (savings increasing). The same underlying data — spend tracking — produced opposite emotional responses depending on whether a goal existed to contextualise it.

Theme 2 — Goal-setting as an activation mechanism: All 4 retained users set a savings goal within 3 days of sign-up. Only 1 of 6 churned users did. Goal-setting appears to change how users interpret the app's data — from judgment to progress signal. This is the strongest behavioural differentiator between groups.

Theme 3 — No built-in re-entry trigger: Churned users had no consistent reason or moment to return to the app. Retained users had created their own rituals (payday, Sunday planning). The app itself is not providing a trigger — retained users succeeded despite this gap, not because of it.

Theme 4 — Payday as a high-motivation but underserved moment: Four users spontaneously mentioned payday as a moment when they feel motivated to engage with their finances — but the app provides no specific payday experience or prompt.

Theme 5 — Financial anxiety as engagement context: Eight of 10 users used emotional language (anxious, stressed, on top of things, in control) when describing their relationship with money. Whether the app reduces or amplifies that anxiety appears to depend on whether the user has a savings goal.

Top insights:
1. Users who set a savings goal in the first 3 days retain significantly better — goal-setting is not a secondary feature; it is the mechanism that turns the app from a ledger into a progress tool.
2. Spend categorisation without forward-looking context creates a judgment experience, not a control experience — users see what went wrong but not what to do next.
3. Finly has no re-entry trigger of its own — retained users invented one; churned users had nothing to return to.
4. Payday is a natural, self-generated motivation peak that the product is not currently responding to.
5. Financial anxiety is the primary emotional context of use — the app either relieves it (for goal-setters) or amplifies it (for users without a progress frame).

JTBD summary: Primary job appears to be "feel in control of my money without obsessing over it." The app succeeds for users who can see progress toward a goal; it fails for users who see only past spend without a forward-looking frame.

Outliers and edge cases: One retained user had not set a savings goal but remained engaged — described using Finly specifically to track spending for a planned holiday purchase. This may represent a distinct "saving for a specific thing" job that warrants separate investigation.

Research gaps:
- No data on notification engagement — it is unknown whether push notifications drive any return visits
- Variable income users not included — the payday trigger and savings goal dynamics may differ significantly
- Long-term retention dynamics (beyond 30 days) not explored
- Did not ask retained users whether they had previously used a competing app

Confidence level: Moderate — 10 interviews; consistent pattern across both groups; goal-setting finding corroborated by product usage data. Limitation: churned users contacted retrospectively via email; recall bias possible. Sample not demographically diverse.
